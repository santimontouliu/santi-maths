Fundamentals for a semester spent with Viveka Erlandsson on structures on hyperbolic surfaces — how a single negative-curvature metric turns straight lines into a dense, chaotic flow.

01

Why Curvature Changes Everything

A closed surface has a topological invariant, the Euler characteristic χ, and Gauss–Bonnet ties it directly to curvature: the total curvature of any metric on the surface must equal 2πχ.

For a genus-g surface, χ = 2 − 2g. Once g ≥ 2, that number is negative — so the surface cannot be flat, and cannot be positively curved. It must carry a metric of constant curvature −1: a hyperbolic structure.

Negative curvature isn't just a sign flip. It means triangles have angle sums below π, disks of radius r have circumference growing like eʳ rather than r, and there is, in a precise sense, exponentially more room the farther out you go. That extra room is exactly what makes the dynamics on these surfaces so rich.

02

The Poincaré Disk: Straight Lines That Curve

To work with this geometry concretely, model it on the open unit disk. The Poincaré disk model assigns the metric ds² = 4|dz|² / (1 − |z|²)² to the disk — distances stretch out faster and faster as |z| → 1, so the boundary circle sits infinitely far away. It is never actually reached; it's the surface's ideal boundary.

In this model, geodesics — the "straight lines" — are exactly the circular arcs that meet the boundary circle at a right angle (diameters are the special case of a circle with infinite radius). Every isometry of the disk is a Möbius transformation, and the whole hyperbolic plane is simply the universal cover shared by every hyperbolic surface.

The pencil of arcs beside this text are all geodesics through a single point — each one locally straight, each one visibly bent, purely because we're looking at a curved space through a flat picture.

03

A Surface From a Single Polygon

Every closed hyperbolic surface can be assembled by gluing the sides of a hyperbolic polygon. The classic genus-2 picture: take a regular hyperbolic octagon whose interior angle at each vertex is exactly π/4, so that eight copies fit snugly around every vertex with angles summing to 2π.

Glue each side to the side opposite it. All eight vertices collapse to a single point on the resulting surface, the edges become four closed loops crossing at that point, and the interior becomes a smooth genus-2 surface with curvature −1 everywhere.

The side-pairing maps generate a discrete group of isometries — a Fuchsian group Γ — and the surface is literally the quotient 𝔻/Γ. Repeatedly reflecting the octagon through its own images tiles the whole disk, the same way a single tile of wallpaper repeats to cover a wall — except here the tiles shrink as they approach the boundary, an artifact of the disk model rather than the surface itself.

04

Geodesic Flow: Where the Dynamics Live

Now set a point moving in a straight line on the surface. Lifted to the disk, that path is just a single geodesic — but the surface keeps folding it back through the fundamental octagon over and over, at a new angle each time it re-enters through a glued edge. This is the geodesic flow: the basic dynamical system every hyperbolic surface carries for free.

Because curvature is negative, two geodesics that start out almost parallel separate at an exponential rate — sensitive dependence on initial conditions, the signature of chaos. The two rays beside this text begin a fraction of a degree apart and are already crossing different octagons entirely.

Push this further and you get one of the field's classical theorems: on a compact hyperbolic surface, the geodesic flow is ergodic — almost every trajectory winds around densely and equidistributes across the whole surface. Making that statement precise, and asking how it varies as the hyperbolic structure itself varies over Teichmüller space, is where this semester's course picks up.
