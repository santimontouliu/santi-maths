01 — Stability

Differentiable Dynamical Systems

Structural Stability,
from Fixed Points to Chaos

Following Lan Wen's Differentiable Dynamical Systems — how the search for a robust notion of stability leads, almost against expectation, straight to chaos.

01

When Is a System Stable?

Two dynamical systems are topologically conjugate if a homeomorphism h translates one into the other: h ∘ f = g ∘ h. Conjugate systems have identical orbit structure — the same picture, just relabeled.

A system f is structurally stable if every nearby system is conjugate to it — small perturbations can't change the qualitative picture, only deform it. It sounds like a mild request. It turns out to be extraordinarily restrictive.

Watch a center — a fixed point surrounded by closed orbits — take an arbitrarily small push: the closed loops break into a spiral. The orbit structure genuinely changes, so a center is never structurally stable. A saddle, pushed the same way, is still visibly a saddle afterward. That difference is the whole subject in miniature.

02

Hyperbolic Fixed Points

The saddle survives because it's hyperbolic: the linearization Df at the fixed point has no eigenvalue on the unit circle. That single condition splits the tangent space into a stable subspace Eˢ, where nearby points contract toward the fixed point, and an unstable subspace Eᵘ, where they expand away from it.

In two dimensions this gives exactly three pictures: both directions contracting is a sink, both expanding is a source, and one of each is a saddle. The portrait beside this text morphs continuously through all three as you scroll.

The Hartman–Grobman theorem says this linear picture isn't just a local approximation — near a hyperbolic fixed point, the true nonlinear system is topologically identical to its linearization. The simple picture is the whole truth, locally.

03

The Smale Horseshoe

Hyperbolicity at a single point is tame. Smale's discovery was that it scales up to something wild. Take a square Q, stretch it long and thin, fold it into a hairpin, and lay it back down across Q. The image f(Q) now crosses Q in two thin vertical strips — everything else in Q gets mapped clean outside it and never returns.

Points that survive every iterate, forward and backward forever, are exactly the points that keep landing back in one of those two strips — and their strips, and their strips' strips. The survivors form a Cantor set: the nested skeleton growing on the square as you scroll.

Code each surviving point by which of the two strips it occupies at every iterate, and the horseshoe becomes exactly the shift map on bi-infinite sequences of 0s and 1s. That's the source of everything that follows: infinitely many periodic orbits, none of them removable by perturbation, and sensitive dependence on initial conditions — chaos, sitting inside a structurally stable system.

04

The Solenoid Attractor

The horseshoe's Cantor set sits inside a bigger space — points can still wander off and never come back. The solenoid uses the same stretch-and-fold mechanism to build a set that actually attracts.

Take a solid torus and map it inside itself by winding it around the core circle twice while shrinking its cross-section and re-centering it. Iterate: the solid torus nests inside itself forever, thinner and more tightly wound each generation, doubling how many times it winds around at every step.

The intersection ⋂ₙ fⁿ(V) — the limit of that infinite nesting — is the solenoid: locally an interval crossed with a Cantor set, wound densely around the core. Nearby points really do get pulled onto it. Same mechanism as the horseshoe, same infinite complexity, but now it's what everything else in the solid torus is drawn toward.
