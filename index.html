Topological Data Analysis

Persistent Homology,
from Point Cloud to Shape

A short, visual walk through how topology finds structure that geometry alone cannot see.

01

The Messy Reality of Data

Real datasets rarely arrive as clean equations. A sensor array, a genomic sample, a manifold of images — each is a cloud of points sitting in some high-dimensional space, distorted by noise, missing values, and measurement error.

Classical geometry asks where things are — exact distances, coordinates, curvature. Topology asks a coarser but more durable question: what shape does this data have, regardless of how it's stretched, bent, or slightly perturbed? A coffee cup and a donut are the same to a topologist; a circle squashed into an ellipse is still a loop.

This resilience to noise is exactly what makes topology a powerful lens for data that was never going to be clean in the first place.

02

The Epsilon Proximity

To recover shape from a discrete set of points, we need a rule for when two points count as "connected." The simplest idea: grow a ball of radius ε around every point, and connect two points as soon as their balls touch.

At ε = 0, every point is isolated — just dust. As ε increases, points begin to merge into clusters, then those clusters merge into larger structures, and loops and cavities start to appear where the data thins out.

The entire story of persistent homology is really the story of watching this single parameter, ε, sweep from zero to infinity — and recording what appears, and what disappears, along the way.

03

Building the Simplicial Complex

Balls and overlaps are intuitive, but to compute anything we need combinatorial structure. Enter the simplicial complex: a scaffold built from vertices (0-simplices), edges (1-simplices), triangular faces (2-simplices), and their higher-dimensional analogues.

Whenever k+1 points are mutually within ε of one another, we fill in the k-simplex that spans them. This is the Vietoris–Rips complex — a purely combinatorial object built directly from pairwise distances, with no coordinates required.

The result is a discrete, computable approximation of the continuous shape the data was sampled from — a mesh assembled entirely out of proximity.

04

Counting the Holes

Once we have a simplicial complex, we can count its holes — and topology gives each dimension of hole a name: the Betti numbers.

  • β₀ — the number of connected components.
  • β₁ — the number of independent loops, or one-dimensional holes.
  • β₂ — the number of enclosed voids, or two-dimensional cavities.

A torus — the point cloud animating beside this text — has β₀ = 1 (it's one connected piece), β₁ = 2 (a loop through the hole, and a loop around the tube), and β₂ = 1 (it encloses a single cavity). These three integers are a compact fingerprint of shape.

05

The Bridge to Rigorous Algebra

Betti numbers feel geometric, but they fall out of pure algebra. Simplices of each dimension form a chain group, connected by a boundary operator ∂ₖ that maps each k-simplex to the signed sum of its faces.

Cycles are chains with no boundary — the kernel Zₖ = ker ∂ₖ. Boundaries are cycles that already bound something — the image Bₖ = im ∂ₖ₊₁. A hole, precisely, is a cycle that is not a boundary:

Hk = Zk / Bk

The k-th homology group. Its rank is exactly βₖ. Persistent homology tracks how these groups evolve as ε grows — which holes are born, which die, and which persist long enough to be signal rather than noise.
